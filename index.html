Perception · 02

Which Orange Circle Is Bigger?

Decide first. Hands up: left is bigger, right is bigger, or the same?

AskCompare the two orange circles. No ruler yet — go with what you see.
RevealThey are identical. The rings fade and the lines show the same width.
WhyYour visual system judges size by comparison with neighbours — a smart strategy in a 3-D world.
CarefulThis doesn’t mean vision is “broken”, and the effect varies from person to person.

Evidence · Massaro & Anderson (1971); Roberts, Harris & Yates (2005)

Perception · 03

One Sound. Three Hearings.

The same short recording plays three times — only the caption changes. Vote each round: what did you hear?

AskHands up per option after each listen. Keep your answer private between rounds.
RevealSame recording, three rounds — votes often shift with the caption. Expect a split: nobody is “broken”.
WhyListening blends the sound with what you expect. With ambiguous input, expectation can decide what you hear.
CarefulThis is a classroom illustration of the classic “green needle / brainstorm” study (Beyerstein, 1988), not a replication. The recording stays ambiguous on purpose — nobody is “broken”, and not everyone hears the same thing.

Evidence · Beyerstein (1988, “green needle / brainstorm” speech-priming study) · one identical embedded recording, replayed unchanged

Attention · 04

Count the White Ball’s Bounces

For 12 seconds, silently count how many times the WHITE ball hits a wall. Ignore the dark disc.

White bounces: 0
AskEyes on the arena. Count the white ball’s wall bounces only. Keep the number to yourself.
RevealThe white ball hit the walls 6 times. Did you also notice the dark star drifting across the middle, and the border turning from slate to crimson?
WhyWhen attention is busy, unexpected things can go unnoticed. In one study, 20 of 24 radiologists searching lung scans missed a gorilla image — and 12 of those 20 looked right at it.
CarefulNot everyone misses these, and it isn’t carelessness. Not noticing is different from forgetting.

Evidence · Simons & Chabris (1999); Drew, Võ & Wolfe (2013); Mack & Rock (1998)

Memory · 05

Memorize the Words

Words will appear one at a time. Read silently — don’t write, don’t speak.

Press start. Words appear for about a second each.

Which of these were on the screen?

Hands up for each word you remember seeing.

TIRED BLANKET SLEEP WINDOW
AskAfter the list: show of hands for each word. Did you see it?
Reveal“Sleep” was never shown. Every word you did see points toward it.
WhyMemory keeps the gist, and the gist can feel like a real memory. In the original studies, many people falsely recalled the missing word — about 40% in free recall — and in recognition tests they said “yes” to it about as often as to words actually shown. Rates in one room will vary.
CarefulA hand up for “sleep” isn’t a bad memory — it’s normal, gist-based memory.

Evidence · Roediger & McDermott (1995); Deese (1959); Gallo (2010) · classroom demonstration, not an exact replication

AskPredict: how far apart will the average guesses be for “contacted” and “smashed”?
RevealExperiment 1 (45 students, one crash film each): average estimates rose with verb intensity — contacted 31.8 · hit 34.0 · bumped 38.1 · collided 39.3 · smashed 40.8 mph. A nine-mph gap from a single word.
WhyA question can carry information, and that information can blend into what we think we remember.

Judgement · 07

Which Evidence Would You Check First?

Hypothesis: “This study method improves test scores.” Tap an evidence card to inspect it. What would you look at first?

AskMost people reach for the supportive cards first. Count how many hands pick A or B before revealing.
RevealYou inspected what agreed with the claim. The contradicting cards sat untouched — until now.
WhyConfirmation bias is the tendency to seek, interpret, or remember information in ways that support existing beliefs.
CarefulThis is a classroom illustration, not a validated psychological test. Nothing here measures anyone’s honesty or intelligence.

Calibration · 08

Confidence Does Not Guarantee Accuracy

Feeling sure and being right are different things — but they are not opposites. Predict each claim, set your confidence, then reveal.

01

Confidence is a feeling, not a measurement

It reflects how smoothly the story fits together in your head — not how much evidence you checked.

Koriat 2012
02

A clean first impression can be informative

Under fair conditions, confidence can carry useful information about accuracy. Reliability depends on how the judgement was made and what happened afterwards.

Wixted & Wells 2017
03

Illusions and nudges can inflate certainty

Misleading context, divided attention, leading questions and untested assumptions can make a wrong answer feel as sure as a right one — like confident hands for the missing word.

Habits · 09

How to Check Yourself

Knowing about biases isn’t enough. Changing the process helps.

SeeingJudges by surroundings→Measure it, don’t eyeball it: a ruler, a number, a second look.
AttentionFilters out the unexpected→Don’t multitask when it matters; pause and scan on purpose.
MemoryFills gaps with what fits→Write it down early, and ask neutral questions, not leading ones.
JudgementTests only for “yes”→Ask what would prove you wrong — then test that.

These habits reduce errors in studies; they don’t remove bias. We spot bias in others more easily than in ourselves.

Evidence · Lord, Lepper & Preston (1984); Pronin, Lin & Ross (2002); Milkman, Chugh & Bazerman (2009)
