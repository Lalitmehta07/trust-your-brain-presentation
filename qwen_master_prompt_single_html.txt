## 5. Required ten-slide narrative

Implement all ten slides as real, working presentation content.

### Slide 1 — Perception: brightness contrast
Title: *Your Brain Does Not Simply Record Reality.* Show two visually identical squares with the exact same fill colour, `#7A7A7A`, against different surrounding backgrounds, one dark and one light. Provide an interactive reveal making equality obvious, such as a connecting bar or aligned comparison. Explain that perceived brightness depends partly on context, not just the physical stimulus. The squares must have identical underlying colours; do not fake the effect with different fills.

### Slide 2 — Perception: the Ebbinghaus illusion
Show two target circles of equal diameter surrounded by different-sized inducers. Follow the brief's geometry where practical: target radius 42 SVG units, one target surrounded by six large inducers of radius 85, the other by eight small inducers of radius 18. Arrange them so the illusion is legible and nothing overlaps or clips. Add a reveal control that fades the inducers and displays measurement guides, demonstrating equal target diameters. Explain visual context and relative size judgments.

### Slide 3 — Perception: ambiguous figures
Create a recognizable Duck-Rabbit ambiguous figure using original inline SVG paths. Let the audience explore both interpretations with explicit controls and contextual cues. Do not rely on an external image. Explain how one stimulus can support different interpretations and how expectations/context can influence perception.

### Slide 4 — Attention: selective-attention challenge
Create a playable timed visual challenge lasting about 12 seconds. Ask the audience to count bounces of a white circle moving through a clearly defined arena. Include unexpected anomalies where feasible: a star crossing the scene, a change in arena border, and a dark disc changing into a square. Afterward, let viewers submit or reveal their count. Provide replay mode highlighting anomalies and explaining why visible events can be missed while attending to another task.

Requirements: local animation; no network-dependent timing; start only on request; replay/reset; no uncontrolled animation after pausing or switching slides; reduced-motion alternative without making the task impossible. Do not claim this is a standardized attention assessment.

### Slide 5 — Memory: false memories and the DRM paradigm
Introduce memory as reconstructive, not a perfect recording. Present these words individually, approximately 1.2 seconds each: Bed, Rest, Awake, Tired, Dream, Wake, Snooze, Blanket, Doze, Slumber, Snore, Yawn. Do not include the critical lure “Sleep”. Then present a recall/recognition poll: Tired, Blanket, Sleep, Window. Allow selections and reveal which words were presented and which was the critical lure. Explain the DRM paradigm and distinguish this classroom illustration from a controlled research experiment.

### Slide 6 — Memory: question wording and recollection
Build an interactive exploration of the Loftus and Palmer 1974 experiment. Allow comparison of the verbs Contacted, Hit, Bumped, Collided, Smashed. Explain how wording influenced participants' estimated vehicle speeds. Distinguish first-experiment estimated speeds from follow-up glass-question findings. The brief identifies 31.8 mph for “contacted” and 40.8 mph for “smashed” in the original speed-estimation experiment; verify these against the source material. Preserve relevant original percentages for false glass reports in the follow-up and identify comparison groups accurately. Do not imply wording arbitrarily rewrites all memories or that every eyewitness account is unreliable.

### Slide 7 — Judgement: Wason 2-4-6 task
Create a functional rule-discovery game. Show sequence 2, 4, 6. Tell viewers it follows a rule and allow them to submit triples to test hypotheses. Give informative feedback about whether each triple fits the hidden rule without revealing it prematurely. The rule is **any three numbers in ascending order**. Allow testing, discovery, and reveal. Explain confirmation bias, hypothesis testing, and disconfirming evidence. Do not accept only even numbers or sequences increasing by two.

### Slide 8 — Judgement: confidence versus accuracy
Create an interactive demonstration of confidence calibration. Use a small, comprehensible set of questions/predictions or confidence judgments. Let viewers record confidence before revealing answers, then compare confidence and correctness. Visually distinguish confidence, actual accuracy, and calibration. Use illustrative data or viewer responses, clearly labelled; do not invent findings or present this as a validated psychological assessment. Explain that confidence is not the same as accuracy.

### Slide 9 — Practical habits for better reasoning
Present four actionable, evidence-informed habits: (1) separate observations from interpretations; (2) protect attention and reduce avoidable distractions; (3) record important details before later information can contaminate recollection; (4) consider alternatives and seek disconfirming evidence. Use a concise before/after comparison or interactive checklist. Give a realistic example for each habit. Do not promise any habit eliminates bias.

### Slide 10 — Synthesis and Q&A
Synthesize: perception is influenced by context; attention is selective; memory is reconstructive; judgement improves when we test assumptions. Emphasize that the brain is adaptive, not defective, and that predictable limitations can be managed through better habits and environments. Provide a final audience question, clean Q&A mode, and a restart control.
