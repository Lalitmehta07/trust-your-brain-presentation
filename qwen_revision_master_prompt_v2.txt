## 3. Revised ten-slide plan

### Slide 1 — Brightness contrast (redesign)
Use two physically identical target patches with exactly the same fill colour, one against a distinctly dark surround and one against a distinctly light surround. Make them large and visible at classroom distance. Do not fake the effect by using different target colours. Add a reveal that removes/masks the surrounds and places both patches side by side on one neutral background, proving their physical equality. Explain context-dependent brightness perception. If the illusion is subtle on a particular display, the equality reveal must still teach the point clearly.

### Slide 2 — Ebbinghaus illusion (preserve)
Keep the current successful orange target circles, surrounding circles, geometry and reveal/measurement interaction. Only fix genuine bugs.

### Slide 3 — Auditory perception: one audio, multiple heard responses (replace Duck-Rabbit)
Remove Duck-Rabbit artwork and controls completely.

Build a Brainstorm / Green Needle-style perceptual-priming demonstration. The central requirement is ONE audio recording only. The exact same recording must be replayed in every round. Never switch recordings, alter playback speed, equalization, or waveform between rounds.

Sequence:
1. Unprimed listen: neutral screen, play the clip, ask what people heard.
2. Visual prime 1: display “BRAINSTORM”, replay the exact same clip.
3. Visual prime 2: display “GREEN NEEDLE”, replay the exact same clip.
4. Reveal that the recording never changed; only the visual expectation changed. Poll the audience after each round.

This is NOT a dichotic listening task. Do not use two speakers, simultaneous voice streams, or different recordings.

Use a verified, legally usable audio recording supplied by the user or otherwise legitimately available. Do not hotlink or scrape copyrighted audio. If no suitable source audio is available, implement the full slide UI and clearly state that the user must supply/embed one recording; do not fake the famous illusion with two synthesized phrases. The final HTML must embed the same audio asset locally as a data URI so it works offline. During the experiment, pause/duck BGM and action sounds. Do not overlay narration.

Explain that auditory expectations can influence perception and that not everyone necessarily hears both interpretations.

### Slide 4 — Selective attention (simplify)
Use ONE primary white ball, a slower predictable path, and about 5–7 clear wall bounces over 10–12 seconds. Ask the audience to count only those bounces. Add only one or two unexpected events, introduced separately, not several simultaneous distractions. Let the audience submit a count, then replay the same sequence with anomalies highlighted. Provide start/replay/reset. Stop timers and animations when leaving the slide. This is an illustration, not a standardized test.

### Slide 5 — DRM false memory (preserve)
Keep the working list, one word at a time:
Bed, Rest, Awake, Tired, Dream, Wake, Snooze, Blanket, Doze, Slumber, Snore, Yawn.
Never present “Sleep” in the list. Poll Tired, Blanket, Sleep, Window. Reveal presented words versus the critical lure. Preserve the caveat that this is an illustrative classroom activity, not a controlled replication.

### Slide 6 — Loftus–Palmer (major redesign)
Make the sequence easy to follow:
1. Show a simple locally rendered car-collision animation.
2. Explain that all groups saw the same collision.
3. Display: “About how fast were the cars going when they ___ each other?”
4. Let the audience tap CONTACTED, HIT, BUMPED, COLLIDED, or SMASHED.
5. Explain that the original study used different wording with different participant groups; one participant did not hear all five versions.
6. Reveal a clear chart of original estimated speeds by verb.
7. Explain the follow-up glass question separately and accurately.

Verify every numerical value and comparison group against the research brief/original study. The brief identifies 31.8 mph for “contacted” and 40.8 mph for “smashed” in Experiment 1; verify intermediate values. Do not confuse Experiment 1 speed estimates with Experiment 2 glass reports. Explain in plain language: what participants saw, what wording changed, what was measured, what the result suggests, and what it does not prove. Do not overstate the study.

### Slide 7 — Confirmation bias (replace Wason)
Remove the 2-4-6 game, number sequences, triple-entry controls and hidden-rule puzzle.

Create a very simple, neutral, non-political visual activity. Show a plausible claim/hypothesis and several evidence cards, some supporting and some challenging it. Ask the audience to tap what they would inspect first. Reveal selected supporting evidence, then reveal overlooked contradictory evidence. Explain that people can preferentially seek or interpret evidence that supports an existing belief. End with: “What evidence would change my mind?” Make it understandable within seconds and touch-first; do not present it as a validated psychological test.

### Slide 8 — Confidence versus accuracy (interactive)
Ask one or two simple questions with unambiguous answers. Before revealing each answer, let the audience choose a confidence level using large touch controls. Reveal correctness and compare confidence with accuracy. Clearly label the tiny activity as illustrative, not a psychological assessment.

### Slide 9 — Practical habits (preserve/polish)
Retain:
1. Separate direct observations from interpretations.
2. Protect attention and reduce distractions.
3. Record important details before later information contaminates recollection.
4. Consider alternatives and seek disconfirming evidence.
Keep it concise and visual.

### Slide 10 — Synthesis/Q&A (preserve/polish)
Retain: perception is context-influenced; attention is selective; memory is reconstructive; judgement improves when assumptions are tested. Keep Q&A and experiment replay shortcuts.
